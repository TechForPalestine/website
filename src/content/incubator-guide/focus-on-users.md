The most important thing to remember is that you are trying to be a high-impact project. If you build the world’s best product or website or whatever, and nobody uses it, then it doesn’t matter how good it was.

The most important thing at the start of the project is to identify your users, and learn from them.

## Users / distribution first

Looking back after your project has achieved great success, it will be clear that it was achieved by connecting a great product to users who needed it a lot.

Working backwards we see that users’ need is central to what you are doing, and that the “great product” derives from what users needed.

However, the real success will not have come just from the users or the product, but from figuring out how to connect them together, how to get the great product to the users. This is called “go-to-market strategy”.

Your aim throughout the early part of the project is to figure out this puzzle:

- who are my target users
- how can a product **like mine** help them
- how can I reach them at scale

Often we hear some variation “we’ll build the product, then we’ll get someone to do marketing for it”. No! This is how projects fail — you’ll waste your time, the time of volunteers and the people who help you, and ultimately fail to help Palestine in any way.

## Identify the users

If you don’t know who your users are, that should be your first step. Lots of projects will think “everyone is our user”, and that’s going to kill your project. Identify the first niche group that will use your product. Perhaps they are the people you are capable of reaching, the group who are most interested in what you offer at the start (we call them early adopters), etc. Your early product and conversations should be tailored to them. A good example of a niche is “students who take part in encampments in US universities” - it’s not all students, it’s not all encampments, it’s a specific sub-niche that you can reach and tailor the product or tool to them.

## Learn from your users (“customer development”)

You should aim to speak to users (**NOTE: not surveys, not focus groups, SPEAK TO THEM)**, so that you can learn a lot about them. Why are they interested in your concept? What excited them? Why did they agree to talk to you? What do they do now instead of the thing you do? What have they experienced that led them to you? Maybe they won’t talk to you — why not?

(why not surveys? because they can only answer questions you think to ask. You want to draw information out from potential users so you can truly understand them. When you have a new question you didn’t think to ask them, you should have learned enough to be able to adopt the persona of a user you spoke to, and answer the question based on this).

(Why not focus groups? Firstly they have the same problems as surveys. Secondly, the entire group gets moved as one — the first person to suggest something anchors the conversation there — and you actually learn less than speaking to a single person. You want 1-1 conversations)

Note in particular that your goal is not to show them the product and ask “would you use this?”. In fact, any question where they might think to give you the answer you want to hear is suspect. Ask open ended questions that lets you learn how they think about the product area.

Bad questions:

- Would you use this? (shows demo)
- Do you need an app that does X?

Good questions:

- “Do you think about X? How are you addressing that?”
- “If I tell you I’m trying to solve X, what does that bring to mind?”
- “Do you use any product or service that does X already?”
- “When you need to do X, how do you go about it?”
- “Are you someone in [a demographic, e.g. organizers, activists for Palestine, etc]”?
- “Can you walk me through a time when you used this product/feature”
- “If you could change one thing about [product/service/feature], what would it be?”
- Many follow-ons to their first answers

**Example:** you’re building a product to boycott movies, these are bad questions:

- I’m building a product to boycott movies, with features X and Y. Would you use this?

These are good questions:

- How many movies do you watch a month?
- How do you pick a movie?
- Where do you watch movies? (netflix, cinema, etc)
- Who do you watch movies with?
- Who do you discuss movies with?
