## MVP first

Almost certainly, with very very few exceptions, you want to build less. Whatever you’re building, try to reduce the scope by 90% and get that version of it out.

You’re planning a website to automate a process? In that case, do the process by hand using a spreadsheet, with your first users. Building things is slow, and you don’t want to wait 2-4 weeks to put a website together to be able to schedule people, only for the first call to go awry because the process doesn’t work. Do it manually, learn as much as you can.

**Example:** Suppose you’re building a product to look through someone’s bank statements to find products to boycott. You can manually take your own bank statements and go through them and try to figure out what you’d boycott. Anything you’d learn from building the product, you can learn from doing this manually.

To state it a different way, you don’t know what you’re building, so how will you know what to build? You might think you know what to build, because you’re smart like that, but I assure you you are not. You will not know anything until you get users to use it, and then you will learn how wrong all of your assumptions were. So build small, get feedback, iterate.

This is true for almost anything. If you need to get someone to participate in something, you can ask them if they’ll do it before you’ve built it. If not, why not? There’s a critical factor there that you need to learn before building it, or you’re wasting everyone’s time building the wrong thing.

Also note, MVP to your problem may or may not be an app/product, it could be a spreadsheet or an SMS system, or Signal group chat to share information.

## Experiments

Everything you do should be treated as an experiment. The worst thing you can do is have a vision for what you want to build, then build it, and only then bring it to users. Most people who do this find that users don’t want what they built, and worse: the users could very easily have given early feedback that saved you months of time.

Everything is an experiment. your path to market, your target users, the medium you’re using, all are experiments. You build an MVP because you need to experiment. Don’t be committed to any of it - instead treat your approach as an experiment as to whether it might work. If you get signals that it’s not working, there could be a few reasons:

- perhaps it’s a bad strategy, and you need a new strategy (do something different)
- perhaps the implementation of the strategy (eg the tactics) were poor.

**Example:** consider ethicsvc. We originally started as a way of getting VCs to sign a letter supporting a ceasefire. We learned that wasn’t going to happen, and learned about how VCs thought about that. So we pivoted into shaming them, and learned that many people were pissed about their complicity. But it wasn’t until the student movement kicked off that we found an effective strategy. However, even in that strategy, we found it really difficult to get students to adopt it. Does that mean the strategy was bad, or the tactics? We tried a number of different tactics and learned from each, getting feedback from our target users (students) along the way. After a lot of trial and error, we learned enough so that people are excited about what we’re doing.

One lovely advantage of doing experiments is that your experiments never fail: you simply learn from them and do something else.

## Risks in order

The most important experiments to try depend on your risks. What is the biggest risk of everything failing? For Boycat, it might be that a mobile scanner can’t work - a technical risk. For ethicsvc, it’s whether students would adopt our criteria, an adoption risk. For palchat, it’s “who is the market for this”.

You should attack the biggest risk first before moving onto the next risk. There’s some nuance here - it might take a month to validate risk 1, but 6 months to validate risk 2. Maybe start there and run them in parallel.

Note that the risk is rarely “can we build the product”. Anyone can build a product with enough time.

Often, you’ll lie to yourself about the risks, and work on the thing you’re most comfortable with. This is because we like to do things we’re comfortable with, and don’t like to do things we aren’t comfortable with. But the risks rarely lie where we’re comfortable — after all, that’s the part we understand the most. Engineers in particular need to stop writing code and instead focus on getting users — which is hard, but is much more important as the risk of “can I build it” is much lower than the risk of “will anyone care”.

## User feedback

The first time you speak to users, they will give you a sense of what you need. Do not build what they tell you to build.

Instead, take their knowledge and beliefs, and try to synthesize them together from multiple users, along with your own intuition and knowledge. Often, you’ll have to throw out some outliers, and say “that person isn’t in our target demographic”. Expect to throw out at least 20% of users, but it’s not uncommon to throw out 50% of users because you’ll find an important niche among the other half that is really valuable.

After that, your goal is to figure out a next step. It might be to build an MVP, or it might be to take the knowledge to build a process (much more likely).

## Iterate, iterate, iterate

After the first few times, you might have an MVP or product for users. Show it to them, have them try it out. Importantly, watch them while they use it, looking both at their face and the screen! You will now learn everything that is wrong, or that you misunderstood the assignment, or a brand new fact that they forgot to mention the first time that’s now a blocker for them. Most likely they will be utterly confused as you what to do, and ask you a ton of questions. It’s best to resist answering their questions, unless they’re so stuck you need to push them forward to learn anything. Take a note of every single thing that went wrong.

After that, you need to iterate. Fix the things that were wrong, broken, didn’t make sense, were confusing, or whatever. Then show it to them again.

The faster you can iterate, the faster you get your product to market. World-leading iteration time is mere minutes or hours. But aim for a 1 day turnaround if you can. If your iteration cycle is 3 weeks, that’s a massive problem, as it usually takes dozens of iterations to get something right.
