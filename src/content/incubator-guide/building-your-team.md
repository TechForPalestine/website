Building a team is probably the biggest thing that can change your project's trajectory.

It's tempting to recruit only for roles you can name, such as "a mobile engineer to build the app". But we've found that adding dedicated, motivated people can be extremely valuable even when you don't yet know what they'll do.

When someone joins, delegate to them. Give them valuable, important work. It would almost certainly be faster to do it yourself, but your goal is to add an extra lane to the highway. Aim to bring on three or four generalists you trust to do important work well.

Once a few people are working with you, the advantages are huge. Someone can take the new opportunity that just arrived. People with context can help you out of indecision. Someone can own a whole product or growth area, freeing you to do the things only you can do.

## How T4P helps you find volunteers

We have over 3,000 volunteers in our database, keen to help. They can lead parts of your project, do regular work such as coding, coordination or research, become a co-founder or partner, or take on one-off research and data entry.

Once you're in the incubator, you request volunteers through the T4P Hub. Our volunteering team helps you work out what you need, advertises the role, searches our database and checks candidates against your basic requirements. You then interview them and decide.

- **Ongoing help:** volunteers who commit for three months or more. Most people in our community have 1 to 5 hours a week.
- **One-off or specialist help:** subject matter experts for a single task, such as professional advice, user testing, design updates or data entry. For small, self-contained tasks like user testing, you can post a Mighty Mission for the whole community to pick up.

When you write a role, be clear about the tasks and responsibilities, separate must-have skills from nice-to-haves, and say whether beginners are welcome. Enthusiastic learners can be a great fit.

## Working with volunteers

**Volunteering is a hobby, so treat it like one.** People stay because this is how they enjoy spending their time. It's social, like volunteering at a soup kitchen or joining a local board. Your volunteers will come back because they make friends here. Treat people as friends and colleagues, and get to know them.

**Meet regularly.** Set up a regular team meeting. In its early days, the ethicsVC team met for an hour three times a week. People are busy, so if an hour every week or two works better, do that.

**Pick a place to talk.** Most projects use a Signal or WhatsApp group, or their own chat server, rather than their public T4P channel.

**Make it easy to add people.** Limit access to sensitive information, such as user data or the identities of partners, until volunteers have built up trust. Set up your permissions so you can bring someone in on day one without that trust.

## Keeping volunteers engaged

### Recruiting

- **Reply fast.** Aim to reply to candidates the same day or the next. Quick replies show you're engaged and stop ghosting before it starts.
- **Double-check availability.** Our volunteering team checks it, but confirm people still have time before you assign work.
- **Be honest about the work.** Don't downplay what your project needs. If it isn't a match, that's fine: give kind feedback and send them back to the volunteering team, who will find them another role.
- **Talk before you decide.** A short voice call builds rapport and makes sure you're aligned. Then give the candidate a day to decide.

### Onboarding

- Welcome new volunteers within their first few days, with live introductions, short guides or videos, and a team meeting.
- Show them the tools your project uses. Not everyone is an expert.
- Ask them which days and times they'll set aside for the project.
- Write a first-week checklist so they know where to start, and give them a first task straight away. Waiting leads to drifting away.

### Making it meaningful

- Walk people through their role and set clear short-term goals.
- Give every volunteer something to own, however small. Responsibility builds commitment.
- Share wins and show your project's real-world impact. It keeps morale high.

### Communication

- Check in regularly, daily or weekly, in your team's chosen channel.
- Keep scheduling flexible. It's the biggest concern for many new volunteers.
- Share your own availability, say when you'll be away, and ask your team to do the same.
- A casual "Hey, how's it going?" can bring back someone who's drifting.
- Ask for feedback, for example with a quick "what's working, what isn't?" session every few weeks, and act on it.

## Screening volunteers

Screening protects your project and the whole T4P community.

- **Pick the right format.** Use video or voice calls for sensitive or long-term roles. A text chat may be enough for one-off tasks. Keep it short: around 30 minutes for important roles and 15 for simple tasks. If you're filling several of the same role, consider a group information session.
- **Respect privacy.** Ask candidates whether they have any preferences or limits on how you connect. Voice-only calls, text interviews and initials-only profiles are all fine when safety is a concern.
- **Set expectations upfront.** Explain your project's goals, the time commitment, any emotional demands and your team culture, and leave time for questions.
- **Ask about motivation and experience.** For example: "What motivates you to support this cause?", "Have you done similar work before? What did you learn?", "Are you comfortable with this time commitment and with sensitive content?" and "What support would help you succeed?"
- **Decide on what matters.** Do their skills match your core needs, or do you have time to help them learn? Do they share your project's values? Passion can matter more than polish: dedicated beginners grow into roles.
- **Verify for sensitive roles.** For very sensitive positions, you can ask for a CV, a LinkedIn profile or references after the first conversation.
- **Confirm in writing.** Give the candidate a day or two to reflect, then ask them to confirm their commitment by a set date.

### Volunteer privacy

Some volunteers want to protect their privacy. T4P supports working under a pseudonym in public, and never attributes someone's work publicly without their consent. To keep the community safe, volunteers share their real identity with T4P's management and volunteering teams and with the project leaders who interview them, and nobody else. Some roles may need ID verification through a trusted third party. As a project leader, you are trusted with this information and must keep it private.
